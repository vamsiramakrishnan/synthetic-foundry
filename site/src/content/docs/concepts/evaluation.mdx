`worldloom validate` answers "does the corpus agree with itself".
`worldloom evaluate` answers a different question: is it hard to retrieve
from.

```bash
worldloom evaluate ./corpus
```

Every world generates its own evaluation set from its own facts. A case can
carry expected fact IDs, required artifacts, explicit distractors, a temporal
cutoff, and an abstention expectation. Grading is mechanical: did the
retrieved passages carry the needed fact IDs, did the top hit predate the
cutoff, did its authority rank hold up. There is no judge model anywhere in
the scorer, so the score is reproducible.

## The question families

A seed-8128 retail build mints 51 cases across eight families. Each family
demands a capability plain keyword overlap does not have.

| Family | Cases | What it demands |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `direct_lookup` | 9 | One fact, one document. The floor: a baseline should pass. |
| `numerical_comparison` | 8 | Several values read together, from more than one passage. |
| `citation_required` | 8 | The answer paired with where it is recorded. |
| `authority_resolution` | 6 | Several documents state the same thing; only one is the current record. |
| `cross_artifact` | 4 | Facts in documents of different types, read jointly. |
| `causal_multi_hop` | 3 | Failure, cause, and consequence strung together. |
| `temporal_state` | 3 | What was believed at time T, before the correction. |
| `expected_abstention` | 10 | Questions the corpus does not answer. The right response is silence. |

## The baselines

BM25 is the default retriever: under a hundred lines, untuned, no notion of
time, authority, or provenance. It exists to fail informatively. On the corpus
above it scores 26 of 51: every direct lookup, and zero of ten abstentions,
zero of three temporal-state cases, zero of six authority cases.

That shape is the result, not a caveat. A baseline that passes the lookups and
fails the temporal, authority, and abstention families is the corpus working
as designed. A rising baseline score is bad news: unless someone improved the
retriever, it means the corpus got easier. CI asserts the hard families stay
hard.

## Two lexical retrievers

```bash
worldloom evaluate ./corpus --retriever both
```

TF-IDF cosine is a genuinely different ranking family from BM25, not the same
formula with new constants. `both` runs them side by side and reports, per
family, whether the corpus is consistently hard, consistently easy, or the two
disagree. A family both fail is hard for the whole word-overlap idea, not for
one heuristic's blind spot.

## Lexical against semantic

```bash
worldloom evaluate ./corpus --retriever all --vectors ./corpus/vectors.json
```

BM25 and TF-IDF are two families and one idea: relevance is word overlap.
`all` adds a dense retriever and names, per family, one of four readings:

- **genuinely hard**: lexical and semantic both fail. The result worth
  having.
- **lexical trap**: the semantic retriever solves it. The family was never
  difficulty, and a corpus card counting it overstates itself.
- **semantic blind spot**: an exact token a dense vector smooths away.
- **solved by everything**: the floor families, as expected.

The dense retriever needs `pip install "worldloom[embeddings]"`. Without it
the dense column is skipped with a message. Its vectors are pinned to a model
revision and cached as quantised integers, so the measurement
[replays offline](/worldloom/concepts/determinism/).

## Exporting the answer key

```bash
worldloom evals export ./corpus -o evals.jsonl
worldloom stats ./corpus
```

`evals export` writes one case per line, sorted keys: the format to score an
external retrieval system against. `stats` reports what the corpus contains:
document counts, lengths, vocabulary, citation graph, near-duplicate rate.
There is no published, auditable figure for "a real enterprise corpus", so
`stats` reports rather than grades. The one comparison it makes is `--against`
a second corpus that also exists.
